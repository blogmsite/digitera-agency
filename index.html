Blog 01 • Social Media

Why posting daily brings no customers

SEO title: “Why posting daily brings no customers” (38 chars) • Meta: “Posting every day but getting no enquiries? The problem is rarely volume. Here is what actually turns posts into customers.” (124 chars)

Posting every day feels productive, but volume is not a strategy. If every post is a random photo, a festival greeting or a screenshot of your work with no caption, no offer and no next step, people scroll past — politely, every day. Attention only becomes an enquiry when three things line up: the post speaks to one specific customer's problem, it proves you can solve it, and it ends by telling the reader exactly what to do next.

Fix the pattern, not the frequency. Plan a week around three posts that matter: one problem-and-solution reel, one proof post (a real job, a real process, a real result you can show), and one direct offer post. Three posts with hooks, captions and a call to action will outperform thirty posts with none of them.

Blog 02 • Video Ads

What makes a UGC ad actually sell?

SEO title: “What makes a UGC ad actually sell?” (35 chars) • Meta: “A UGC ad sells when it feels real: real hands, real use, one clear benefit and a clear next step. Here is the structure that works.” (130 chars)

UGC works because it does not look like advertising. The viewer sees a real person, in a real setting, using the product the way they would use it themselves. The moment it looks staged — studio lighting, scripted lines read off a card — trust drops and so do sales. Our 38-second colouring-book ad follows the honest pattern: the book in real hands, pages being coloured on screen, calm and satisfying to watch.

The structure is simple: open on the product in use (never a logo), show one clear benefit in action, let a real reaction or detail do the persuading, and finish with one call to action. Caption everything — most people watch with the sound off, and a captioned UGC ad keeps its argument even in silence.

Blog 03 • Video Editing

3-second reel mistakes that kill views

SEO title: “3-second reel mistakes that kill views” (39 chars) • Meta: “Most reels lose their viewers in the first 3 seconds. Slow starts, buried hooks and tiny captions are the usual causes — fix all three.” (136 chars)

A reel is decided before it properly starts. Mistake one: the slow intro — a logo animation, a “hey guys” greeting, or three seconds of walking towards the camera while nothing happens. Mistake two: the buried hook — the interesting claim appears at second nine, after most viewers have already left. Mistake three: captions nobody can read — small, low-contrast text placed where the platform's own buttons cover it.

The fix costs nothing but discipline: start on the strongest frame, put the hook in words on screen in the first second, keep captions big and high-contrast, and cut every shot that does not earn its place. Editing is mostly removal — what you cut in the first three seconds decides whether anyone sees the rest.

Blog 04 • Websites

How to brief a website that launches on time

SEO title: “How to brief a website that launches on time” (45 chars) • Meta: “Websites launch late when the brief is vague. Pages, content, examples and one decision-maker — what to prepare before build starts.” (134 chars)

Websites rarely launch late because of coding. They launch late because nobody decided what the pages were, the text arrived in fragments, photos were “coming soon” for six weeks, and three different people gave three different opinions on the homepage. A good brief removes all four risks before a single page is built.

Prepare five things: the list of pages you need; the real text for each page (even rough — rough text on time beats perfect text late); your logo, photos and brand colours in one folder; two or three sites you like, with what you like about them; and one named person who approves things. With those in place, design, build and launch can follow the simple three-step flow shown in our How We Work section — without the drift.

Blog 05 • Writing

Copywriting vs content writing: which do you need?

SEO title: “Copywriting vs content writing: your guide” (43 chars) • Meta: “Copywriting persuades; content writing informs and ranks. Most businesses need both — here is how to tell which job you are hiring for.” (137 chars)

The two get confused because both are “writing”, but they do opposite jobs. Copywriting is written to make someone act now: a landing page, an ad, a sales email, the words on a button. It is short, direct and judged by enquiries and sales. Content writing is written to be found and trusted over time: blog guides, help articles, LinkedIn posts. It is judged by rankings, readers and the enquiries that arrive weeks later.

Ask what the piece must do. If a page must convert a visitor who is already looking at it, you need copywriting. If you need the right visitors to find you in the first place, you need content writing — ideally SEO content, planned around real searches. Most growing businesses need content to bring people in, and copy to turn those people into customers.

Blog 06 • LinkedIn

LinkedIn ghostwriting for founder enquiries

SEO title: “LinkedIn ghostwriting for founder enquiries” (44 chars) • Meta: “Founders win enquiries on LinkedIn without posting daily. Ghostwriting turns your experience into a steady, credible presence.” (128 chars)

Buyers check the founder before they reply to the company. A LinkedIn profile that posts nothing — or posts only company announcements — quietly loses enquiries that were almost won. But most founders do not have an hour a day to write, and forcing it produces posts that sound like everyone else.

Ghostwriting solves the time problem without faking the voice. The process is an interview, not invention: your real opinions, real lessons and real customer stories are drawn out, then written up in your words, on a steady rhythm. Two or three strong posts a week, plus thoughtful comments on the right people's posts, build the familiarity that turns “we've been connected for months” into “let's talk”. You approve everything before it posts — it is your name on it.

Blog 07 • SEO

SEO for a new website: the first 30 days

SEO title: “SEO for a new website: the first 30 days” (41 chars) • Meta: “New site, no rankings yet? The first 30 days of SEO are about structure, titles, internal links and one useful page a week. Start here.” (137 chars)

A new website does not rank in week one, and anyone who promises that is selling you something. What the first 30 days can do is remove every obstacle between your site and the rankings it will earn. Week one: technical basics — the site loads fast, works on mobile, is submitted to Google Search Console, and has a sitemap and correct canonical tags.

Week two: every page gets a unique title and meta description written around a real search, and your service pages link to each other sensibly. Weeks three and four: publish one genuinely useful guide a week — answering a question your customers actually ask — and link each guide to the service it supports. That rhythm, kept up, is what compounds. SEO is a structure you build once and a habit you keep.

Blog 08 • Content

Turn one video into a week of content

SEO title: “Turn one video into a week of content” (38 chars) • Meta: “One good video can become a reel, three posts, a blog section and a week of stories. Here is the repurposing system we use.” (124 chars)

Businesses run out of content because they treat every post as a new production. One well-planned video — a product demo, a founder tip, a before-and-after — contains a week of material if you take it apart deliberately. The full video is the anchor asset; everything else is an extraction.

From one 60-second video: the best 20 seconds become a standalone reel with a new hook; three stills with the video's key lines become feed posts; the transcript, lightly edited, becomes a short blog section that helps SEO; two short vertical crops become stories with a question sticker; and the strongest single sentence becomes a text post. Plan the extractions before you film, and one shoot fills the calendar instead of one slot in it.

Blog 09 • Branding

Brand design people remember and trust

SEO title: “Brand design people remember and trust” (40 chars) • Meta: “Memorable brands are consistent, not complicated. Colours, type and layout used the same way everywhere is what builds trust.” (126 chars)

People rarely remember a clever logo. They remember a business that always looks like itself: the same colours, the same type, the same clean layout on the reel, the quote, the website and the invoice. Recognition is built by repetition, and trust follows recognition — a business that looks consistent looks established, even when it is small.

That is why a brand kit matters more than a single beautiful design. Colours with exact values, two fonts used properly, templates for the posts you make every week, and rules simple enough that anyone on your team can follow them. With that system in place, every new piece of content strengthens the brand instead of diluting it — including an animated version of your logo that opens your videos the same way, every time.

Blog 10 • Marketing Budget

How much should a small business spend on marketing?

SEO title: “How much should a small business spend on marketing?” (53 chars) • Meta: “There is no magic percentage. Spend follows your goal, your margin and your channel — here is a calmer way to decide your budget.” (131 chars)

Percentages get quoted everywhere, but a percentage of the wrong number is still the wrong number. A business with healthy margins selling a high-value service can justify spending far more to win one customer than a low-margin shop selling small items. The honest starting point is not a percentage — it is the value of one customer, and what you can afford to pay to reach them.

Then spend in order. First, the assets everything else depends on: a website that converts and brand visuals that look trustworthy. Second, one channel you can sustain — for most small businesses, consistent short-form video or SEO content. Third, only after the first two work, paid ads to amplify what is already converting. At DigiTera every engagement starts with a scoped quote against a defined goal, so the budget follows the outcome you need — not a package you may not.

There is no fixed price list — the work is scoped to your goal. You receive one clear written quote before anything starts, work begins only after you approve it, and nothing beyond the agreed scope is charged without your written agreement first.

What affects the price of a quote?

The number of deliverables, how much raw material you already have (footage, photos, text), how fast you need it, and how many revision rounds and formats are included. Your quote itemises this, so you can drop or add items before approving.

How long does work take?

It depends on scope and on how quickly feedback and materials arrive from your side. Short video work is normally turned around in days; a website follows the design → build → launch flow, with the launch date agreed in the quote before we start. Have a fixed deadline? Tell us in the brief — we will scope to it honestly, or say plainly if it cannot be done well in that time.

What do I need to supply?

Your logo and brand colours if you have them, any footage or photos you want used, access details for accounts we manage for you, and one person who can approve work. If you do not have footage, photos or text yet, say so in the brief — planning and creating them can be included in the scope. The fuller checklist is in our blog guide, “How to brief a website that launches on time”.

How do revisions work?

Every quote includes an agreed number of revision rounds. A revision is a change to work already delivered within the agreed scope — colours, wording, cuts, layout. New deliverables or a change of direction are a scope change, quoted separately before we do them. We would rather agree this in writing at the start than surprise you later.

Who owns the finished work?

Once the project is paid in full, the final delivered assets — videos, designs, website files and written content created for you — are yours to use for your business. Working files and templates we use across clients remain ours unless your quote says otherwise, and anything with a third-party licence (stock, music, fonts) stays under its licence terms, which we flag in the quote.

Do you guarantee results — followers, rankings or sales?

No, and you should be careful with any agency that does. We guarantee the work itself: the deliverables in your quote, made properly, on the agreed schedule. Rankings, reach and sales depend on your market, your offer and time — we build the content, structure and consistency that give them the best chance, and we refine based on what your audience actually responds to.
